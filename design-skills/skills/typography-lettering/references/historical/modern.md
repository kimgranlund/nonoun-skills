# Modern / Didone Types (1780–1830) — historical reference

The roman types cut between roughly 1780 and 1830 in France, Italy, and Germany — by Firmin Didot in Paris, Giambattista Bodoni in Parma, Justus Erich Walbaum in Weimar, and their contemporaries — are the most visually extreme serifed letters Western typography has produced. They push stroke contrast to the limit of what metal, paper, and ink could hold; they rationalize construction to the point of mechanical regularity; and they collapse the calligraphic rhythm of the humanist and transitional traditions into a fully abstract, fully vertical system of thick stems and hairline serifs. This is the category Vox-ATypI calls **Didones** (a portmanteau of **Did**ot + B**od**oni) and Bringhurst calls **Neoclassical** or **Romantic** types.

This file is the medium-coverage historical reference for the Modern / Didone era. It is not the Modernism of Bauhaus, de Stijl, or the International Typographic Style — that is a 20th-century art-historical usage unrelated to the 18th/19th-century typographic category documented here. For the classification-level treatment see `../classification/vox-atypi.md`; for the preceding era (Baskerville, Fournier) see `./transitional.md`; for the humanist and Garalde beginnings see `./humanist-renaissance.md`; for the industrial-era reaction that follows see `./slab-egyptian.md`.

A note on terminology: "Modern" in a 19th-century French, Italian, or British type catalog means *moderne* / *modern-style* — the Didot-Bodoni look — and this is the sense preserved in Vox (*Didone*), DIN (*Klassizistische Antiqua*), and BS 2961 (*Didone*). "Modernism" in 20th-century graphic design history refers to a separate movement centered on sans-serif, grid composition, and the rejection of historicist ornament — see `./sans-grotesque.md` and the International Typographic Style in that file. The two senses of "modern" do not connect.

## The Didot family

The Didots were a French printing dynasty of more than two centuries — printers, publishers, punchcutters, paper-makers, and typographers — whose accumulated output defines French typography from the mid-18th through the mid-19th century. The relevant generation for Modern type runs from François-Ambroise Didot (1730–1804) to his sons Pierre Didot *l'aîné* (1761–1853) and Firmin Didot (c. 1764–1836).

### François-Ambroise Didot (1730–1804)

The patriarch. A printer, publisher, and punchcutter working in Paris, François-Ambroise is the Didot who altered the standard of French type design in two ways that propagated across Europe:

- **He pushed stroke contrast** further than Baskerville or Fournier had — the first real move toward the Didone extreme. His types from the 1770s already show finer hairlines than any of his French contemporaries and set up the direction his son Firmin would carry to completion.
- **He revised the typographic point system.** In 1783 François-Ambroise standardized a point system tied to the *pied du roi* (the royal foot, a French legal standard of length), defining 1 point = 1/72 of a French Royal inch ≈ 0.376 mm. This is the **Didot point**, the unit of type measurement used across continental Europe through the late 20th century and still a reference in some European type contexts. It supersedes Pierre-Simon Fournier's earlier (1737) point system. Note that 1783 is the canonical date; the system is sometimes informally referred to as "c. 1770s" because François-Ambroise had been refining it through that decade. In 1973 the Didot point was restandardized to 0.375 mm (= 3/8 mm) within the EU, though Berthold's earlier 1878 standardization at ~0.376 mm remained in use for much of the 20th century. The Anglo-American pica-point system (1 pt = 1/72 inch ≈ 0.353 mm) is a separate, later convention — see `../metrics/metrics-glossary.md` on typographic point.

### Firmin Didot (c. 1764–1836)

The youngest son, and the punchcutter who produced the types now called "Didot" in both printing and font catalogs. Firmin began cutting types around 1784, and his 1784 specimen showed unprecedentedly thin hairlines — strokes so fine that printing them cleanly required the best paper, ink, and press work available. His types became the house face of the Imprimerie Royale (later Imprimerie Impériale under Napoleon), and through the Imprimerie they became the French state typographic identity.

Firmin's characteristic letter-level moves, against his father's and Baskerville's transitional models:

- **Stroke contrast raised to roughly 1:10** (thin:thick), against Baskerville's roughly 1:4–1:5 and Garamond's roughly 1:3.
- **Fully vertical stress axis** — all trace of the pen-tilted Humanist/Garalde stress is gone. The `o`'s thin points are at 12:00 and 6:00.
- **Unbracketed, hairline, perfectly horizontal serifs.** The serif meets the stem at a 90° angle with no bracket curve; the serif itself is a thin horizontal bar.
- **Ball terminals on `a`, `c`, `f`, `r`, `y`** — a pen-origin feature preserved decoratively where other strokes have been rationalized.
- **Uniform curve radii** — round glyphs (`o`, `O`, `c`, `e`, `Q`) share a common arc, producing visual regularity across the alphabet.
- **Distinct uppercase forms** — `Q` with a detached, often curling tail; `R` with a tapered leg; `J` descending below the baseline.

## Giambattista Bodoni (1740–1813)

Born in Saluzzo in the Piedmont, Bodoni trained in Rome in the 1760s at the Propaganda Fide press — the Vatican's missionary printing office, which cut exotic-script types (Arabic, Hebrew, Syriac, etc.) and was the premier school of punchcutting in Italy. In 1768 Duke Ferdinand I of Bourbon-Parma appointed him director of the **Stamperia Reale** (Royal Press) in Parma, a position he held for the remaining forty-five years of his life.

Under Ferdinand's patronage — and with Parma's status as a small, wealthy court state with a cultural-prestige project — Bodoni had the resources, stability, and freedom to run a punchcutting shop at a scale and quality no other contemporary could match. He cut on the order of **300 typefaces** over his lifetime (the exact count depends on how sizes and variants are counted), across Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, and exotic scripts. He was the most prolific punchcutter of the early Modern era and arguably the most consequential.

### Design philosophy

Bodoni wrote about his work in the introductory essays of his specimen books. The values he repeatedly names — **regolarità** (regularity), **nitidezza** (cleanness / sharpness), **buon gusto** (good taste), **grazia** (grace) — position his types as the outputs of an aesthetic of mechanical precision. The regularity is visible at the letter level (uniform curve radii, consistent stem widths, rigidly vertical stress) and at the page level (his books are set with generous leading, wide margins, and asymmetric title-page composition). The Bodoni page is a monument to rationalized typography.

Compared to Firmin Didot, whose types are closely contemporary, Bodoni's are often described as slightly warmer — a marginally higher x-height, subtly more generous curves, slightly less aggressive hairlines at equivalent sizes. At the specimen level the two are distinguishable by practitioners; at casual reading level they are nearly interchangeable.

### Manuale Tipografico

Bodoni's *Manuale Tipografico* is the single most influential typography specimen book of the 19th century. A 1788 first edition already showed the scope of his work; the definitive **posthumous 1818 edition** — published in two volumes by his widow Paola Margherita Dall'Aglio Bodoni, with the direction of Luigi Orsi (the Parma press's director) — contained specimens of **over 250 romans and italics in the Latin alphabet**, plus Greek, Hebrew, Russian, Arabic, Phoenician, Armenian, Coptic, Tibetan, and other exotic scripts, borders, ornaments, mathematical and astronomical symbols, and music notation. It was printed in 290 copies, dedicated to Duchess Marie-Louise of Parma (Napoleon's widow, then sovereign of Parma). Daniel Berkeley Updike, in *Printing Types* (1937), called it "probably the most elaborate specimen that the world has ever seen." Taschen published a facsimile edition in 2010 and a Bibliotheca Universalis edition in 2016 (Stephan Füssel, ed.).

## Walbaum — the German Didone

Justus Erich Walbaum (1768–1837) was a punchcutter working first in Goslar (from 1796) and then in Weimar (from 1802). His types were cut from the late 1790s through the 1810s and 1820s — roughly contemporary with Firmin Didot and slightly later than Bodoni's mature work.

Walbaum's types are **more robust** than the French and Italian Didones: slightly wider proportions, marginally less extreme contrast, somewhat broader serifs. The letterforms retain a trace of humanist warmth that pure Didone suppresses. This robustness is part of why Walbaum was favored for **German-language text** in the 19th and early 20th centuries — German orthography uses long compound words, German readers were accustomed to Fraktur's visual density, and Walbaum's slightly heavier Didone texture sat more comfortably in that context than the delicate French Didone.

Walbaum's business was inherited by his son Theodor in 1828, but Theodor died unexpectedly in 1836 and the foundry was sold to the publisher F. A. Brockhaus; Brockhaus later sold the materials to H. Berthold AG in 1918, which is the route by which Walbaum types reached 20th-century German typography.

Walbaum was eclipsed in popular use during the late 19th century (when Fraktur was the dominant German book face for vernacular text) and during the early 20th century (when old-style serifs based on pre-1800 models were in fashion). The 1920s Monotype revival program brought Walbaum back as a viable modern Didone choice.

## Key design features — the Didone formula

A Didone is recognizable by a small number of intersecting features. Any Modern face will exhibit most of these; a face missing several of them is probably transitional or something else.

- **Stroke contrast of roughly 1:10 or more** (thin hairline to thick stem). Compare transitional (~1:4–1:6), Garalde (~1:3), humanist Renaissance (~1:2). The Didone hairline is a true hairline — a line near the limits of what a printing press can hold.
- **Fully vertical stress axis.** The thinnest points of a round glyph (`o`, `O`) are at the top and bottom, not tilted. The pen is gone.
- **Hairline serifs — unbracketed, horizontal, and thin.** The serif is a flat line perpendicular to the stem, not a triangular wedge or a bracketed fillet. In Didot this is most extreme; Bodoni has slightly more bracket; Walbaum has the most.
- **Ball terminals on `a`, `c`, `f`, `j`, `r`, `y`** (varies by face). These decorative rounded terminals are a surviving trace of the pen's round-tip ink-bloom from the Transitional era, preserved as an ornamental touch on the otherwise rationalized letter.
- **Uniform curve radii.** Round letters share similar arcs. This produces a strongly regularized visual rhythm that reads as "constructed" rather than "drawn."
- **Distinct uppercase features.**
  - `Q` with a detached tail (often a separate stroke curling below or through the bowl).
  - `R` with a tapered, curving leg that flicks outward.
  - `J` descending well below the baseline.
  - `G` with a spur where the bar meets the bowl (Bodoni more than Didot).
- **Relatively low x-height compared to cap-height.** Didone types look "small" at a given `font-size` compared to contemporary UI sans — a visible consequence of the classical proportion the Didones retained from the transitional tradition. See the **Metric issues** section.

## Technology — why hairlines became possible

The Didones could not have existed fifty years earlier. A set of late-18th-century printing-technology advances made the extreme contrast printable at all.

- **Improved paper.** Smoother, calendered, less absorbent papers (notably the wove paper developed in the 1750s by James Whatman and spread to continental European paper mills by the 1780s) held hairlines without bleeding. Baskerville's 1750s types had already required smoother paper than Garamond-era rough laid sheets; Didones pushed this further.
- **Higher-quality ink.** Cleaner ink formulations with better pigment dispersion produced sharper transfer from punch to paper.
- **Better punchcutting precision.** Steel tooling improved through the 18th century; Firmin Didot and Bodoni both worked to standards of precision that earlier punchcutters — even Baskerville's team — could not match.
- **Earlier iron-frame and later rotary presses.** The Stanhope press (iron-frame, c. 1800) allowed more even impression pressure than wooden-frame hand presses. Rotary press development in the first third of the 19th century drove newspaper printing and, eventually, made Didone body text workable at high volume — though by then the legibility compromises (below) were becoming evident.

Without these preconditions, Firmin Didot's 1784 hairlines would have filled with ink, broken on press, or vanished into the rough paper. The Didone style is a response to, and a showcase for, the new tooling.

## The legibility problem

Didones are **display-optimal and body-text-suboptimal**. At display sizes — headlines, covers, posters, inscription-like titling — the extreme contrast reads as elegance, refinement, sharpness. At body-text sizes, the same contrast becomes a legibility liability in three ways:

1. **Hairlines disappear.** At 9pt print, let alone 12–14px screen, the thinnest strokes drop below the perceptual threshold or the rendering resolution, breaking the letter's structure.
2. **"Dazzle" at normal reading sizes.** The alternation of very thick stems and very thin hairlines creates a vibrating visual texture that tires the eye on long passages — particularly in harsh lighting or at marginal contrast.
3. **Apertures close down.** As hairlines get finer, the apertures on `c`, `e`, `s` become relatively small compared to the stem weights, reducing glyph distinguishability.

Despite this, Modern types were used for body text throughout 19th-century French and Italian publishing — novels, essays, scholarly editions. Contemporary taste for the style overrode the legibility compromise. By the mid-19th century the reaction is visible in the **Clarendon / slab-serif / Ionic** tradition (see `./slab-egyptian.md`) which takes the Didone's rationalized construction but restores a low, uniform stroke contrast for newspaper and book-body use.

Modern optical-size cuts compensate. ITC Bodoni Six, Bauer Bodoni Book, Linotype Didot Text, Walbaum Text — all have stouter hairlines, lower contrast, and higher x-height than the display cuts, making them usable at body sizes. Using a display cut for body text is an anti-pattern (see below).

## Contemporary usage

The Didone style has a specific cultural semantic in 2026 that is broadly consistent across usages.

- **Fashion magazine identity.** *Vogue* uses a proprietary Didone (the "Vogue" wordmark is based on a Didot-derived letter). *Harper's Bazaar* identity is explicitly HTF Didot (the 1991 commission that created the face). *Elle*, *Marie Claire*, *Vanity Fair*, *W* magazine all use Didones or Didone-derived headline faces. Fashion-magazine typography is one of the strongest surviving domains of the Modern style.
- **Luxury and fashion branding.** Giorgio Armani, Valentino, Tiffany & Co., Ralph Lauren, Chanel, and many other luxury brands use Bodoni-like or Didot-like wordmarks and wayfinding. The Didone signals "high-culture heritage + sharp elegance."
- **Upscale editorial and invitations.** Wedding invitations, museum invitations, gallery announcements, literary-imprint display — Didones are the default "classic elegant" option.
- **Editorial headlines.** *The New York Times*'s op-ed and feature headline face (Cheltenham, transitional) is not a Didone; but various *Times*, *New Yorker*, *Atlantic*, and similar publications use Didone-derived display faces for flagship piece headlines.

**Not suitable for:** UI body text, dense information design, small-print, poor-rendering environments. Using a Didone for 14px body text on a low-DPI screen will produce a page of shimmering, fragile letters that tire readers. For body-text Didone work on screen, always select a text-optical-size cut (Bodoni Six, Didot Text, Walbaum Text) and verify hairline survival at the target rendering resolution.

## Metric and CSS considerations

A few practical notes for using Didones in modern typography, and particularly on the web.

### Low x-height relative to cap-height

Didones inherit classical proportions from the Transitional era: x-height roughly 0.43–0.48 × em, cap-height roughly 0.68–0.72 × em. Compared to contemporary UI faces (Inter ~0.55, SF Pro ~0.56, Segoe UI ~0.55), Didones look visibly smaller at the same `font-size`. Pairing a Didone display headline with a contemporary UI sans body at the same `font-size` makes the headline appear too small. Either **bump the Didone's `font-size` by ~10–15%** or apply **`size-adjust`** via `@font-face` to normalize. See `../contemporary/metric-overrides.md`.

### Hairline fragility on low-DPI screens

At 96 DPI (standard desktop monitors pre-Retina), Didone hairlines can drop below a single pixel in width at body sizes and disappear or become fragile. Modern high-DPI displays (Retina, 4K) render hairlines faithfully at 2× and higher. When delivering Didones for web, assume some users will be on low-DPI displays and verify hairline survival there. Font-smoothing CSS (`-webkit-font-smoothing: antialiased`) helps but does not fully compensate.

### Optical-size pairing

Where a Didone ships with optical-size variants, use them. HTF Didot's 7 cuts (6, 11, 16, 24, 42, 64, 96) are designed to be used across body-to-billboard. ITC Bodoni's three cuts (6, 12, 72) span body, caption/deck, and display. Linotype Didot's text cut ships alongside the display cut and should be specified for body sizes. In CSS, either select the optical-size cut by `font-family` name, or — where a variable version ships — wire the `opsz` axis explicitly. See `../techniques/optical-size.md` and `../contemporary/variable-fonts.md`.

### Synthetic bold is an anti-pattern

A Modern face depends on a specific, designed weight ratio between hairlines and stems. Synthetic bold (browser `font-weight: bold` applied to a regular cut) uniformly thickens all strokes, which **destroys the contrast**. Where bold weights are needed, ensure a designed bold cut is loaded. If none is available, use a different face.

### Metric compatibility with paired sans

For Didone + sans pairing (a common editorial pattern — Didone display over sans body), choose a sans with a compatible cap-height-to-x-height ratio, or plan on `size-adjust`. Humanist and geometric sans with tall classical proportions (Futura, Avenir, Trajan derivatives) sit comfortably with Didones; neo-grotesques with high x-height (Helvetica Neue, Inter) will fight the Didone unless metric-compensated.

## Parma, Parmigianino, and heritage branding

Bodoni's patron was Ferdinand I of Bourbon-Parma, and Parma's state press under Bodoni was one of the cultural prestige projects of 18th-century Italian court patronage. Parma preserved Bodoni's equipment, specimens, and archive; the **Museo Bodoniano**, founded in 1963 within the Palatina Library of Parma, holds the most complete surviving collection of Bodoni matrices, punches, and printed works. Parma's contemporary cultural identity — tourism, food culture (*prosciutto di Parma*, *Parmigiano-Reggiano*), the Parma Conservatory — retains Bodoni as a local heritage reference; some Parma municipal wayfinding and tourist materials use Bodoni or Bodoni-derived typography deliberately.

This is the most specific case of "Didone as civic heritage" but not the only one: Weimar retains Walbaum as local heritage, and Paris retains the Didots as part of the French state typographic identity (the Imprimerie Nationale was still using Didot derivatives into the late 20th century).

## Didone italic — a distinct genre

Didone italics deserve separate treatment because they are meaningfully different from Garalde or Transitional italics and are widely mis-specified.

The Didone italic is **highly sloped** (12°–16°, steeper than most Garaldes), **narrow** (condensed relative to the roman), and has **its own construction grammar** — not a sloped roman. Key markers:

- **Ball-terminal entry strokes** on lowercase `a`, `c`, `d`, `f` — more ornate than Garalde italic entry strokes.
- **Sharp, pointed serifs at stroke ends** — on `n`, `m`, `h`, the exit stroke terminates in a fine point, contrasting sharply with the thick stems.
- **Cursive `k`, `v`, `w`, `z`** — these glyphs pick up flourishes (a curled leg on `k`, swash descenders on `y`, sometimes looped `z`) that are absent from the roman.
- **Single-storey `a`** (vs the roman's double-storey form).
- **Condensed proportion.** A Didone italic set at the same point size as the roman occupies noticeably less horizontal space — a legacy of the italic's origin as a space-efficient companion (see `./humanist-renaissance.md` on the 1501 Griffo italic).

Firmin Didot's italics were the first mature Didone italics and set the pattern for Bodoni, Walbaum, and their 19th-century descendants. The italic is always cut as a distinct set of punches, not as a sloped roman — and a contemporary Didone revival without a proper designed italic (only a synthetic oblique) is an incomplete family. When evaluating a "Bodoni" license, check the italic cuts: ITC Bodoni 6 / 12 / 72 all have full designed italics, as does HTF Didot and Bauer Bodoni; some cheaper "Bodoni" redistributions use slanted romans and should be avoided for editorial work where italic emphasis matters.

## Scotch Roman and the 19th-century British descendants

The Didone style did not stop at Didot, Bodoni, and Walbaum. A parallel British tradition — **Scotch Roman** — develops in Edinburgh foundries from around 1810 onward, led by **William Miller** (Miller & Richard foundry) and **Richard Austin** (who had earlier cut Bell for John Bell in 1788). Scotch Roman retains the Didone formula — vertical stress, high contrast, fine serifs — but with slightly more bracket on the serifs, a heavier hairline, and a more robust text fit than Didot's Paris or Bodoni's Parma work. The Scotch Roman is the **text-workable Didone** of the 19th-century English-speaking world; it was used widely in British and American book printing through the mid-19th century and survived in American newspaper body typography into the 20th.

Relevant descendants and revivals:

- **Monotype Scotch Roman** (1907, Monotype drawing office) — the mechanical-composition revival that made Scotch Roman widely available.
- **Caledonia** (W. A. Dwiggins, Mergenthaler Linotype, 1938) — an American revival in the Scotch Roman tradition; Dwiggins's specific reworking, widely used in mid-century American book printing. Caledonia is arguably the most successful 20th-century Didone adaptation for sustained body-text reading.
- **New Caledonia** (1982) — the ITC-licensed redraw with extended weights.
- **Miller** (Matthew Carter, Carter & Cone, 1997) — a contemporary Scotch Roman revival. Miller Display, Miller Text, and Miller Banner (different optical sizes) give a complete family. Widely used in 2000s–2020s editorial, including some *New York Times* sections.
- **Escrow** (Cyrus Highsmith, Font Bureau, 2006) — another contemporary Scotch-tradition face, used in *Wall Street Journal* redesign work.

The distinction between "a Scotch Roman" and "a Bodoni" is useful: Scotch is text-grade Didone with practical concessions (fatter hairlines, more bracket); Bodoni is display-grade Didone in its most-characteristic cuts. When a design calls for "Bodoni-like at body size," the right answer is often a Scotch Roman like Miller Text, not a text cut of Bodoni.

## Modern no. 20 and the British-Modern lineage

In addition to Scotch Roman, 19th-century British typography produced an idiom called **Modern** (British, capital-M) that sits between Didone extreme and Scotch text-grade. The canonical **Monotype Modern** series — **Modern Extended**, **Modern Wide**, **Modern Condensed**, and especially **Modern no. 20** — dominated British general-purpose typography from the 1870s through the 1920s, when Monotype's revival program brought Bembo, Garamond, Baskerville, and Times New Roman into circulation. Modern no. 20 is essentially a British Didone stabilized for general commercial book and periodical printing — higher x-height than French Didot, softened hairlines, reasonable italic.

Modern no. 20 is not widely used today but appears in facsimile and 19th-century-pastiche contexts. Its name collides with the contemporary English adjective "modern" and with the 20th-century graphic-design "modernism," which is another reason "Didone" is the preferred term.

## Didone decline and 20th-century marginality

Between roughly 1850 and 1920 the Didone was the dominant French and Italian book face — in France almost exclusively (via the Imprimerie Nationale and major literary publishers), in Italy widely (via Bodoni's successors and regional foundries). It was not dominant in the English-speaking world after about 1860: Caslon-revival and Garalde-revival romans (reissued Caslon, Monotype Garamond, ATF Century, Cheltenham) pushed Didone out of British and American book printing by the turn of the 20th century.

By the 1920s, the **Monotype revival program** — Stanley Morison's editorial direction of Bembo (1929), Garamond (1922), Baskerville (1923), Bell (1931), Times New Roman (1932), Walbaum (1934), and others — established a vocabulary of "good book types" heavily weighted toward Humanist, Garalde, and Transitional models. Didone was present in this revival (Monotype Bodoni 1911, Monotype Walbaum 1934) but as a minority category. Serious 20th-century book printing gravitated toward Bembo, Garamond, Baskerville, Sabon, Dante, and later Minion — not Didone.

The Didone's niche through most of the 20th century was therefore **display** — fashion magazines, luxury branding, theatrical posters, formal announcements. This niche is still the Didone's home in 2026. Attempts to revive Didone as a serious book-text face (e.g., certain Penguin Great Ideas editions set in ITC Bodoni Six, some French literary imprints with Linotype Didot Text) are possible but uncommon.

The parallel rise of **sans-serif body text** in the second half of the 20th century — particularly Helvetica and Univers for corporate identity, and screen-first sans like Verdana, Georgia, Inter, and SF Pro for UI — further narrowed Didone's territory. A 2026 web or app interface almost never uses a Didone, and when it does, it is in a deliberate editorial-fashion or luxury-brand context rather than a general UI choice.

## Display / text contrast and the optical-size tradition

Bodoni himself cut different type designs for different sizes — not just different point sizes cast from the same punches, but **distinct designs** for different usage contexts. A Bodoni cut at 48pt for poster display has different hairline proportion, different serif weight, and slightly different letter construction than a Bodoni cut at 8pt for footnote body. This practice — **optical sizing** — was standard in high-quality metal type from the 15th century through the 19th century and was largely lost in 20th-century phototype and early digital type, where a single "Bodoni" master was typically scaled mechanically to all sizes.

The **ITC Bodoni 6 / 12 / 72** revival (Sumner Stone et al., 1994) was one of the first modern digital attempts to restore this tradition — three distinct optical-size designs, usable at three different size ranges, each drawn from actual Bodoni originals for that size. **HTF Didot** (1991–92) did the same with seven optical sizes. The **Commercial Classics Walbaum 2010** family has multiple optical sizes. These are the contemporary Didone families that take optical sizing seriously.

The alternative — using a single cut across body-to-display — always compromises. A display-weight hairline is too thin for 9pt body; a body-weight hairline is too thick for a 72pt headline. The Didone is a style where optical-size compromise matters more than for almost any other category, because the hairlines are the style.

Variable-font `opsz` axis support for Didones is still sparse as of 2026. Most high-quality Didone revivals (HTF Didot, ITC Bodoni, Bauer Bodoni) ship as discrete optical-size cuts rather than variable-font axes. A few newer releases (some Commercial Classics Walbaum cuts, certain Adobe Fonts Didones) are experimenting with variable opsz, but the practical state of Didone on the web in 2026 is still: select the appropriate discrete optical-size cut by `font-family` name. See `../contemporary/variable-fonts.md` and `../techniques/optical-size.md`.

## Didone in non-Latin scripts

Bodoni himself cut Greek, Cyrillic, Hebrew, Arabic, and other exotic-script types — the 1818 *Manuale Tipografico* includes specimens of Armenian, Coptic, Phoenician, Tibetan, Syriac, and other scripts. The Didone aesthetic — vertical stress, extreme contrast, hairline serifs — was transposed with uneven success across scripts:

- **Greek Didone.** Monotypes of Greek in the Didone idiom were produced in the 19th century and survived into the 20th. The Greek letter's already-high stress axis (Greek lowercase inherits a pen-slanted character less rationalized than Latin) makes Didone Greek feel less radical than Didone Latin. Modern Greek Didones include **Didot Greek** and the Greek companions to HTF Didot and ITC Bodoni.
- **Cyrillic Didone.** Extensive use — the Russian Imperial state press adopted Didot-style romans in the late 18th century (the **Russian civil type** reforms had already Latinized Cyrillic in the Petrine era; Didone Cyrillic was the 19th-century further rationalization). Contemporary Cyrillic Didones include the Cyrillic cuts of Paratype Bodoni, Pragmatica, and various post-Soviet foundry releases.
- **Arabic.** Didone-style Arabic is rare and arguably unidiomatic — Arabic's calligraphic tradition (naskh, thuluth, nastaliq) resists the vertical-stress / extreme-contrast transformation that defines Didone. Arabic editorial typography has developed its own contemporary aesthetics independent of the Latin Didone lineage. See `../scripts/arabic.md`.
- **CJK.** No meaningful Didone tradition exists in CJK. CJK character systems have their own stroke-contrast conventions (Mincho / Ming / Song faces for Chinese and Japanese have stroke contrast analogous to serif Latin, but the calligraphic roots and construction are independent).

A Latin Didone brand rolling out to global markets should plan carefully for how the style translates. Fashion magazines with multilingual editions (Vogue, Harper's Bazaar) typically pair the Latin Didone wordmark with localized script typefaces from distinct design traditions, rather than forcing a Didone transform on the local script.

## Canonical anchor set

For quick reference, the small anchor set of "what is definitively a Didone":

- **Firmin Didot's 1784 specimen onwards** (Paris, France)
- **Giambattista Bodoni's 1790s–1813 work** (Parma, Italy)
- **Justus Erich Walbaum's late 1790s–1820s work** (Weimar, Germany)
- Their contemporaries: **Molé le jeune** (France), **Presse de la Imprimerie Royale** cuts, Parma ducal press cuts, Scotch Roman British contemporaries

And for quick reference, the small anchor set of "contemporary Didone revivals to specify by name":

- **HTF Didot** (Hoefler & Co., 1991–92) — 7 optical sizes — the reference contemporary Didot
- **ITC Bodoni 6 / 12 / 72** (ITC, 1994) — 3 optical sizes — the reference contemporary Bodoni
- **Bauer Bodoni** (Bauer, 1926, Jost) — sharpest extreme-contrast Bodoni
- **Linotype Didot** (Frutiger, 1991) — stable licensed workhorse Didot
- **Walbaum 2010** (Commercial Classics, Paul Barnes) — reference contemporary Walbaum
- **Miller Text** (Matthew Carter, 1997) — Scotch Roman text-grade Didone-adjacent

When in doubt, specify the foundry.

## Digital-revival quality tiers

Summarizing the preceding notes into a practical ranking:

**Accurate / scholarly (license for serious work):**
- HTF Didot (Hoefler & Co.) — the reference contemporary Didot, 7 optical sizes
- ITC Bodoni 6 / 12 / 72 (ITC / Monotype) — the reference contemporary Bodoni, 3 optical sizes
- Bauer Bodoni (Bauer / Neufville / various licensees) — the sharpest, most extreme-contrast Bodoni; preferred for high-end display
- Linotype Didot (Adrian Frutiger, 1991) — stable neutral Didot workhorse
- Walbaum 2010 (Commercial Classics / Paul Barnes) — reference contemporary Walbaum
- Storm Bodoni 26 (František Štorm) — careful size-specific revival
- Berthold Bodoni Old Face (G. G. Lange, 1983) — German-tradition revival

**Decent but compromised:**
- Monotype Bodoni / Bodoni Book (1911) — workable, but hairlines strengthened for small-size hot-metal printing, so the extreme-contrast character is softened
- Didot LT Std — Linotype's baseline Didot licensing
- Walbaum Monotype / Linotype (1934 / 1936) — solid workhorses without the scholarly refinements of 2010-era revivals

**Avoid as "Bodoni" or "Didot":**
- Most free or low-tier digitizations — hairlines typically too thick, proportions compressed, optical-size variants absent, italic companions missing or weak
- Generic "Didoni" releases — caveat emptor

## Anti-patterns and traps

1. **Using a display Bodoni/Didot cut for body text on screen.** Hairlines fragmentation, dazzle, legibility loss. Always select a text cut (ITC Bodoni Six, HTF Didot at 6–11pt cut, Linotype Didot Text, Walbaum Text) for body sizes. If the license doesn't include a text cut, pick a different face for body.

2. **Synthetic bold.** Destroys the designed contrast ratio that defines the Modern style. Load the designed bold cut or skip the weight.

3. **Confusing display and body cuts.** HTF Didot 96 is not usable at body sizes; HTF Didot 11 is not usable at billboard sizes. The optical sizes are not interchangeable — they are parametrically distinct designs. A document that uses one size of HTF Didot at both 14px body and 72px headline is fighting the family's design.

4. **Pairing two Didone display cuts.** A page with two different Didone display faces (e.g., HTF Didot and Bauer Bodoni both at headline size) almost always fights itself — the faces are visually too similar to be read as deliberate contrast but too different to read as unified voice. Choose one Didone per page.

5. **Treating "Bodoni" as a single thing.** Different Bodonis are different designs. A brand guideline that specifies "Bodoni" without naming the foundry leaves the door open for anything from Monotype Bodoni Book (subdued) to Bauer Bodoni (extreme). Always specify.

6. **Ignoring the x-height mismatch when pairing with sans.** Setting a Didone display at the same `font-size` as a UI sans body produces headlines that read as too small. Use `size-adjust` or bump the display `font-size`.

7. **Assuming hairlines survive on low-DPI screens.** Test on 1× (96 DPI) displays before shipping. If hairlines vanish or look fragile, select a cut with stouter hairlines or add an `@media (resolution: 1dppx)` fallback.

8. **Using "Modern" to describe a modernist-style sans-serif layout.** "Modern" in typographic-category usage means Didone; "Modernism" in graphic-design usage means the mid-20th-century Swiss/International Typographic Style. The two are unrelated. Don't conflate them in copy, CSS class names, or design-system naming.
