# DIN 16518 classification — typography reference

**DIN 16518** is the **German national standard for typeface classification**, issued by the **Deutsches Institut für Normung** (DIN — the German standards body) in **1964**. It parallels the Vox-ATypI classification published a decade earlier (Vox 1954, ATypI adoption 1962) but partitions the typographic landscape into **eleven groups** using German-language terminology, with a different handling of sans-serifs, glyphic / variant romans, casual hand, and blackletter. DIN 16518 carries the legal / industrial authority of a DIN standard inside German-speaking typographic and print-industry practice; internationally it is less cited than Vox but continues to appear in German-language type education, foundry catalogs, and printing-trade manuals.

This file is the **light reference** for DIN 16518: the eleven groups, how they map to Vox-ATypI, where DIN is stronger or weaker than Vox, and how to use it in contemporary practice. For the more widely-cited international taxonomy see `./vox-atypi.md`; for the essayistic English-language alternative see `./bringhurst.md`; for the 1921 silhouette-based French predecessor see `./thibaudeau.md`.

## Origin and status

DIN 16518 was issued by the **Deutsches Institut für Normung** in **1964** as part of the DIN standards family covering type, print, and publishing — sibling standards include DIN 16507 (point-size systems) and DIN 16511 (proof marks). The standard was drafted by a committee of German type designers, typographers, and print-industry representatives; its purpose was a shared German-industrial vocabulary for catalogs, specifications, and pedagogy.

Key facts about the standard itself:

- **Issued 1964.** Post-dates Vox (1954) and the ATypI adoption of Vox (1962); predates BS 2961:1967 by three years. The three classifications emerged in quick succession, reflecting a mid-20th-c. desire across European type communities for shared vocabulary.
- **Listed as current at Beuth Verlag** (the DIN standards publisher) as of 2026-04-18, with issue date 1964 and no subsequent revision. DIN 16518 has been **effectively frozen** since the 1960s; updates proposed in the 1990s and 2000s (to handle digital type, non-Latin scripts, contemporary sans subdivisions) have not materialized.
- **Legal / industrial weight inside Germany.** German printing-trade contracts, typesetting specifications, foundry catalogs, and type-design curricula have traditionally referenced DIN 16518 groups directly (Gruppe III, Gruppe VI). Its authority is the authority of a DIN standard — not binding outside regulated contexts, but normative in German professional practice.
- **Reach outside Germany.** Limited. Bringhurst, Lawson, Blackwell, and other English-language type historians cross-reference DIN when comparing classifications but do not use DIN vocabulary as primary. French and Italian type education follows Vox; Dutch and Scandinavian education mixes Vox and Bringhurst; German education continues to reference DIN alongside Vox.

## The eleven DIN 16518 groups

The standard defines **eleven** groups — not nine as in Vox-ATypI's original proposal, not ten as in BS 2961, and distinct from the post-2010 eleven-group Vox revision. The groups in order:

| Group | German name | English approx. | Vox-ATypI equivalent | Anchor examples |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|----------------------|-----------------|
| **I** | Venezianische Renaissance-Antiqua | Venetian Renaissance Roman | Humanes | Jenson, Centaur, Cloister, Schneidler |
| **II** | Französische Renaissance-Antiqua | French Renaissance Roman | Garaldes | Garamond, Bembo, Sabon, Aldine, Minion |
| **III** | Barock-Antiqua | Baroque Roman | late Garaldes + early Réales | Caslon, Fournier, Janson, Van Dijck |
| **IV** | Klassizistische Antiqua | Classicist Roman | Réales + Didones | Baskerville, Didot, Bodoni, Walbaum |
| **V** | Serifenbetonte Linear-Antiqua | Slab-Serif | Mécanes | Rockwell, Clarendon, Memphis, Courier, Serifa |
| **VI** | Serifenlose Linear-Antiqua | Sans-Serif | Linéales (all four subclasses) | Helvetica, Univers, Futura, Gill Sans, Akzidenz-Grotesk |
| **VII** | Antiqua-Varianten | Roman Variants | partial overlap with Incises | Albertus, Copperplate Gothic, Trajan, Friz Quadrata |
| **VIII** | Schreibschriften | Handwriting / Scripts | Scriptes | Snell Roundhand, Zapfino, Bickham, Mistral |
| **IX** | Handschriftliche Antiqua | Handwritten Romans | partial overlap with Manuaires | Klang, Post Antiqua, Comic Sans, Delphin |
| **X** | Gebrochene Schriften | Broken Scripts (Blackletter) | Fractures | Textura, Rotunda, Schwabacher, Fraktur, Kurrent |
| **XI** | Fremde Schriften | Foreign Scripts | Étrangères (added Vox 2010) | All non-Latin |

Group X (**Gebrochene Schriften**) is subdivided in DIN into **five sub-groups** that correspond to historical blackletter traditions — Gotisch (Textura), Rundgotisch (Rotunda), Schwabacher, Fraktur, and Fraktur-Varianten. This five-way split is the finest-grained blackletter subdivision in any major classification and is one of DIN's distinctive contributions; see `../historical/blackletter.md`.

### Gruppe I — Venezianische Renaissance-Antiqua

The **Venetian Renaissance tradition** — types derived from or inspired by Nicolas Jenson's 1470 Venetian roman and the humanist-minuscule-inspired faces that followed. In DIN terms, this captures the Humanist / Jenson sub-tradition specifically (slanted crossbar on the lowercase `e`, slanted axis, low contrast, tall ascenders). Corresponds cleanly to Vox **Humanes**.

Anchor faces: Jenson (Adobe Jenson, LTC Jenson, Centaur MT), Centaur (Rogers 1914), Cloister Old Style (Benton 1897), Schneidler Old Style (F. H. E. Schneidler 1936), Jannon (in the Humanist rather than Garalde reading), Berkeley Old Style.

### Gruppe II — Französische Renaissance-Antiqua

The **French Garalde tradition** — Garamond, Bembo (despite being Venetian-cut by Griffo, DIN treats it as Garalde on stylistic grounds), Aldine revivals, and the 16th–17th-century roman families that descend from Griffo-Garamond-Granjon-Van Dijck. Corresponds to Vox **Garaldes**.

Anchor faces: Garamond (Adobe Garamond, Stempel Garamond, Garamond Premier, Sabon), Bembo (Monotype Bembo, Bembo Book), Granjon, Galliard, Minion, Plantin, Palatino (sometimes; DIN is ambiguous on Palatino's placement — Zapf designed it as a contemporary Venetian-Aldine, so Gruppe II fits, but its contrast pushes toward Gruppe III for some sorters).

### Gruppe III — Barock-Antiqua

The **Baroque Roman** group — Caslon, Fournier, Janson, Van Dijck, and the late-17th / early-18th-c. types that sit between Garalde refinement and Transitional rationalization. This is DIN's most contested bucket: it straddles the Garalde-Réale boundary that Vox places differently. Many faces that Vox classifies as late Garalde or early Réale end up in DIN III, making Gruppe III a **catch-all transitional bucket**.

Anchor faces: Caslon (Adobe Caslon, ITC Founder's Caslon, Williams Caslon), Fournier (Monotype Fournier), Janson (Hermann Zapf's Janson Text), Van Dijck (Monotype Van Dijck), Fleischmann (DTL Fleischmann), Kis (Monotype Ehrhardt — descended from Miklós Kis, late 17th c.).

### Gruppe IV — Klassizistische Antiqua

The **Classicist Roman** — combines what Vox separates into **Réales** (Baskerville, Bell) and **Didones** (Didot, Bodoni). DIN treats Baskerville through Bodoni as a single rationalized-Enlightenment tradition; Vox splits them at the contrast threshold. This is the second-most-contested DIN bucket after Gruppe III.

Anchor faces: Baskerville (ITC New Baskerville, Berthold Baskerville Book), Bulmer, Bell, Didot (HTF Didot, Linotype Didot), Bodoni (Bauer Bodoni, ITC Bodoni 72), Walbaum (Monotype Walbaum 2018), Modern No. 20, Scotch Roman.

### Gruppe V — Serifenbetonte Linear-Antiqua

**Slab-serif / Egyptian** — the 19th-c. commercial display tradition. "Serifenbetont" literally means "serif-emphasized"; "Linear-Antiqua" signals monoline (linear / constant-stroke) weight distribution. Corresponds to Vox **Mécanes**.

Anchor faces: Rockwell (Monotype 1934), Clarendon (Besley 1845), Memphis (Weiss 1929), Courier (Kettler 1955 — monospaced), Serifa (Frutiger 1967), Egyptienne F, Archer, Roboto Slab. The bracketed-Clarendon / unbracketed-Egyptian distinction present in BS 2961 and Vox is not formalized in DIN — both sit in Gruppe V.

### Gruppe VI — Serifenlose Linear-Antiqua

**Sans-serif** — a single bucket for the entire sans tradition. "Serifenlos" = "serifless"; "Linear-Antiqua" signals the absence of stroke-contrast emphasis. **DIN does not subdivide Gruppe VI** — Grotesque, Neo-Grotesque, Geometric, and Humanist sans all land here. This is DIN's single largest weakness for contemporary use; by contrast, BS 2961 introduced the four-way Lineal subdivision (Grotesque / Neo-Grotesque / Geometric / Humanist) which is now the standard vocabulary worldwide.

Anchor faces: Akzidenz-Grotesk, Helvetica, Univers, Arial, Futura, Kabel, Erbar, Gill Sans, Frutiger, Myriad, Optima (contested — Gruppe VI or VII), Inter, Roboto, SF Pro, Avenir, Open Sans, Fira Sans, Söhne.

### Gruppe VII — Antiqua-Varianten

**Roman Variants** — a bucket for **decorative, ornamental, display, and flared-terminal romans** that don't fit the main chronological spine. Covers glyphic / inscriptional faces (Albertus, Trajan), engraved / copperplate styles (Copperplate Gothic), highly-contrasted display romans, and ornamental / flourished types. Overlaps with Vox **Incises** but broader — Gruppe VII is a catch-all for "roman with something unusual" rather than Vox's specifically-glyphic *Incises*.

Anchor faces: Albertus (Berthold Wolpe, Monotype 1932), Copperplate Gothic (Goudy 1901), Trajan (Twombly 1989), Perpetua (Gill 1929 — some sorters place here for its glyphic influence; others in Gruppe II for its serifed roman proper), Friz Quadrata (Friz 1965), Optima (Zapf 1958 — contested between VI and VII), Meridien (Frutiger 1957).

### Gruppe VIII — Schreibschriften

**Scripts** — formal connected handwriting-imitation. Corresponds cleanly to Vox **Scriptes**. Includes both the formal copperplate / Roundhand tradition and more casual connected scripts.

Anchor faces: Snell Roundhand (Carter 1966), Bickham Script (Lipton 1997), Zapfino (Zapf / Siegel 1998), Edwardian Script, Künstler Script (Kuenstler Schreibschrift, Stempel), Shelley, Commercial Script, Mistral (Excoffon 1953 — connects casually; sometimes placed here, sometimes in IX).

### Gruppe IX — Handschriftliche Antiqua

**Handwritten Romans** — informal pen-drawn faces that are roman in skeleton but retain visible hand-drawn character. "Handschriftliche Antiqua" literally "handwritten Roman" or "handwritten Antiqua"; distinguishes itself from Gruppe VIII (Schreibschriften = formal connected scripts) by being **unconnected** and **roman-skeletoned**. This is DIN's distinctive-and-not-found-elsewhere category — Vox has **Manuaires** (casual graphic / hand-drawn faces) but DIN IX is narrower, specifically about pen-drawn roman-esque informal text faces.

Anchor faces: Klang (Will Carter 1955), Post Antiqua (Herbert Post 1932), Delphin (Georg Trump 1951), Comic Sans (Connare 1994 — uncontroversially here in DIN terms), Papyrus (Costello 1982 — often here though DIN does not explicitly place it), Mistral (ambiguous between VIII and IX). Some practitioners push Bradbury Thompson's Mademoiselle, Trump Mediaeval's italics, and certain Hermann Zapf scripts toward IX.

### Gruppe X — Gebrochene Schriften

**Broken scripts / blackletter** — all blackletter traditions, with internal sub-divisions:

- **Gotisch (Textura)** — the earliest, most angular blackletter (Gutenberg 1455 was set in Textura).
- **Rundgotisch (Rotunda)** — rounder Italian-influenced blackletter, dominant in Iberia and Italy through the 15th-c.
- **Schwabacher** — German 15th-c. blackletter with curved strokes, less angular than Textura.
- **Fraktur** — the defining German blackletter 1513 onward, Dürer-Schönsperger collaboration for Emperor Maximilian I. Dominant German script until 1941.
- **Fraktur-Varianten** — display and ornamental Fraktur variants (Walbaum Fraktur, Fette Fraktur 1875, Wilhelm Klingspor Gotisch).

This five-way subdivision is DIN's most detailed bucket and reflects the cultural importance of blackletter in pre-1941 German-language printing. See `../historical/blackletter.md` for the deeper narrative (including the January 1941 Nazi Antiqua Decree that effectively ended Fraktur as the dominant German script).

Anchor faces: Textur (Berthold Textur, Monotype Old English), Fette Fraktur (Berthold 1875), Walbaum Fraktur, Fette Schwabacher, Wilhelm Klingspor Gotisch, San Marco, Cloister Black, Blackmoor.

### Gruppe XI — Fremde Schriften

**Foreign / non-Latin scripts** — a single bucket for every non-Latin script: Greek, Cyrillic, Arabic, Hebrew, Devanagari, CJK, Thai, Hangul, Ethiopic, etc. This is the same kind of placeholder category that Vox added in 2010 as **Étrangères** — note that DIN had this category from 1964, 46 years earlier, though the handling is equally superficial. Non-Latin typography is treated script-by-script in `../scripts/`; Gruppe XI is not a serious classification of it.

## Comparison with Vox-ATypI

DIN 16518 and Vox-ATypI are the two major European classifications of the mid-20th-c. They share broad structure but differ in important ways:

| Axis | Vox-ATypI (1954, 1962) | DIN 16518 (1964) |
|------|------------------------|-------------------|
| Origin | French typographer / ATypI international adoption | German national standards body |
| Groups | 9 (original) / 10 (BS) / 11 (2010) | 11 (from 1964) |
| Language | French names (Humanes, Garaldes, Didones) | German names (Antiqua, Gebrochene) |
| Sans subdivision | 4 (via BS 2961:1967) — Grotesque, Neo-Grotesque, Geometric, Humanist | None — all sans in Gruppe VI |
| Blackletter subdivision | 1 (Fractures) | 5 (Gotisch, Rundgotisch, Schwabacher, Fraktur, Fraktur-Varianten) |
| Glyphic / flared terminals | Incises (distinct category) | Gruppe VII Antiqua-Varianten (broader, includes ornamentals) |
| Casual hand | Manuaires (distinct category) | Gruppe IX Handschriftliche Antiqua (narrower — pen-drawn roman only) |
| Transitional bucket | Réales (Baskerville etc. separated from Didones) | Gruppe IV merges Réales + Didones |
| Baroque handling | None; Caslon splits between Garalde/Réale | Gruppe III Barock-Antiqua explicit |
| Non-Latin | Étrangères (added 2010) | Gruppe XI Fremde Schriften (from 1964) |

The two classifications **overlap substantially** on the main roman chronology (Jenson → Garamond → Caslon → Baskerville → Bodoni), on slabs, and on scripts / blackletter. They **diverge** on:

- **Sans subdivision.** Vox-via-BS 2961 has four sub-buckets; DIN has none. This is the largest practical divergence — DIN is under-specified for contemporary sans-dominated typography.
- **Roman pacing.** Vox distinguishes Humanes / Garaldes / Réales / Didones as four separate categories; DIN merges Réales + Didones (Gruppe IV) and names Baroque (Gruppe III) as its own bucket.
- **Glyphic / variant handling.** DIN's Antiqua-Varianten is broader than Vox's Incises — it captures both glyphic/chiseled faces and ornamental / decorative romans that don't fit elsewhere.
- **Casual hand.** DIN's Handschriftliche Antiqua is narrower than Vox's Manuaires — DIN wants pen-drawn roman-like faces specifically; Vox allows broader graphic / hand-drawn styles.
- **Blackletter.** DIN's five-way subdivision is the most granular in any classification.

### Strengths of DIN 16518

- **Integrates blackletter as a first-class category with granular subdivisions.** Reflects the cultural weight of blackletter in pre-1941 German printing. BS 2961 and Vox treat blackletter as a single bucket; DIN's five sub-classes remain the reference for blackletter taxonomy.
- **Simpler to teach for German-industrial use.** Eleven groups, clear German-language names, legal-standard authority. For a German print shop's catalog or a German design school's curriculum, DIN is the historically-default.
- **Handschriftliche Antiqua as a distinct category** captures a specific Latin-script tradition (informal pen-drawn roman) that Vox collapses into Manuaires. For cataloging faces like Delphin, Post Antiqua, or Klang, DIN IX is more precise than Vox Manuaires.
- **Baroque explicit.** Gruppe III Barock-Antiqua acknowledges a real stylistic era that Vox's Garalde-Réale boundary handles awkwardly (Caslon being the canonical contested case).
- **Non-Latin acknowledged from 1964.** Vox waited until 2010 to add Étrangères; DIN had Fremde Schriften from the beginning. The handling is equally superficial in both, but DIN earns partial credit for acknowledging the gap earlier.

### Weaknesses

- **No sans subdivision.** Gruppe VI pools Helvetica, Futura, Gill Sans, and Inter into a single bucket. For any contemporary use (~80% of new type releases are sans), this collapses meaningful distinctions. BS 2961's four-way Lineal split is universally used in contemporary practice, including by German-language writers who cite Vox + BS 2961 rather than DIN for sans discussions.
- **Fremde Schriften is a catch-all.** Like Vox Étrangères, the single bucket for all non-Latin scripts is a placeholder, not a classification. Serious global typography uses script-specific conventions (naskh / nastaliq / kufi for Arabic; monotonic / polytonic for Greek; Myeongjo / Gothic for Korean); DIN provides none of this detail.
- **German-specific terminology.** The group names (Venezianische Renaissance-Antiqua, Barock-Antiqua, Serifenbetonte Linear-Antiqua) are awkward outside German-speaking contexts. Translating "Gruppe V" to "Slab" is straightforward; teaching "Serifenbetonte Linear-Antiqua" as a term is harder. DIN's reach is bounded by this.
- **Gruppe III (Barock) is fuzzy in practice.** It straddles the Garalde-Réale boundary and ends up as a transitional catch-all. Sorting Caslon into III is uncontroversial in DIN practice; sorting late-Garalde Dutch types (Van Dijck, Kis) is contested.
- **Gruppe IV merges Transitional and Modern.** The Baskerville-Didot-Bodoni merger obscures the real stylistic differences between late-18th-c. Enlightenment rationalism (Baskerville: moderate contrast, bracketed serifs) and early-19th-c. hairline-contrast Didone (Didot, Bodoni: extreme contrast, unbracketed serifs). Vox's separation is more faithful to design history.
- **Frozen since 1964.** The standard has not been substantively revised for digital type, variable fonts, screen-first sans, or global typography. DIN 16518 is effectively a 1960s document still nominally current — its gaps reflect the typographic landscape of that era, not 2026's.

## Classification disagreements

Faces where DIN placement is contested or where DIN and Vox diverge — useful for teaching the seams of both systems:

| Face | DIN (typical) | Alternative DIN | Vox-ATypI | Notes |
|------|---------------|------------------|-----------|-------|
| **Optima** (Zapf 1958) | Gruppe VI | Gruppe VII | Linéale Humaniste or Incise | The canonical multi-system disagreement — flared chisel terminals + humanist proportions defy single-bucket placement |
| **Times New Roman** (1932) | Gruppe III | Gruppe IV | Réale / Garalde hybrid | Baroque or Classicist depending on sorter; reflects the face's own transitional-derivative design |
| **Caslon** (1720s) | Gruppe III | (none — DIN III is stable for Caslon) | contested Garalde / Réale | DIN III is designed for exactly this kind of face; Vox struggles |
| **Courier** (1955) | Gruppe V | (monospace caveat) | Mécane + monospace | DIN V absorbs monospace slabs without a separate handling |
| **Inter** (2016) / **SF Pro** (2015) | Gruppe VI | Gruppe VI with humanist modifier | Linéale — typically Humanist, sometimes Grotesque | DIN VI lacks sub-buckets, so all UI sans collapse together |
| **Roboto** (2011) | Gruppe VI | (none — VI absorbs everything) | Linéale hybrid (Neo-Grotesque / Humanist / Geometric) | DIN cannot express Roboto's mixed DNA |
| **Fira Code** (2014) | Gruppe V | Gruppe VI | Mécane with monospace, or Linéale with monospace | Monospace code fonts are cross-cutting; DIN doesn't formalize mono |
| **Source Serif** (Slimbach 2014) | Gruppe II | Gruppe III | Garalde with Transitional tendencies | Bucket II vs III judgment call; represents the Garalde-Barock boundary |
| **Palatino** (Zapf 1948) | Gruppe II | Gruppe VII | Garalde (some sorters place in Humanes) | A contemporary Venetian-Aldine; DIN II fits, but the calligraphic voice pushes some toward Gruppe VII |
| **Comic Sans** (Connare 1994) | Gruppe IX | (none — unambiguous) | Manuaire | Canonical Gruppe IX face; DIN's Handschriftliche Antiqua exists partly because of faces like this |
| **Fraktur** faces | Gruppe X, sub "Fraktur" | — | Fractures | DIN's five-way blackletter subdivision is the most granular available |
| **Trajan** (Twombly 1989) | Gruppe VII | — | Incise | Caps-only inscriptional — both DIN VII and Vox Incise fit |

## Contemporary relevance

Where DIN 16518 is actually used in 2026:

- **German type education.** HfG Offenbach, Burg Giebichenstein Halle, Hochschule für Gestaltung Karlsruhe, FH Mainz (Willberg's institution), and other German design schools reference DIN 16518 alongside Vox. Typical curriculum presents both systems and notes divergences.
- **German foundry catalogs and specimens.** Older German foundries (Berthold, Linotype-German, H. Berthold AG's successors, URW++) organize specimens by DIN groupings. Newer German-speaking foundries (Dinamo, LettError, Typejockeys) use looser genre buckets akin to the Google Fonts taxonomy.
- **Beuth Verlag / DIN standards.** The standard remains purchasable from Beuth Verlag and carries DIN-standard authority for contracts, specifications, and certifications that cite it.
- **Willberg, *Wegweiser Schrift* (2001).** The standard German-language type-classification textbook; pairs DIN groupings with practical sorting examples and still in print.
- **Cross-referenced in English-language texts.** Bringhurst's *Elements* mentions DIN in the classification discussion (ch. 7); Lawson's *Anatomy of a Typeface* notes DIN in a few contexts; Blackwell's *Twentieth-Century Type* comparatively discusses Vox + DIN. Jaspert/Berry/Johnson's *Encyclopaedia* indexes faces by Vox, not DIN.
- **Not widely used in English-language typographic discourse or web/UI practice.** English-language design writing (Butterick, Rutter, Typographica, I Love Typography) uses Vox / Bringhurst / informal genre buckets; DIN appears only when comparing to Vox.

Practical rule: **if you are working in a German-language context** (catalog, curriculum, specification, contract), DIN 16518 is the relevant standard. **If you are working in English** and your goal is a shared classification, use Vox-ATypI (with BS 2961's Lineal subdivision) as primary and cite DIN only for cross-reference.

## Revision history

- **1964** — Initial publication as DIN 16518:1964, *Klassifikation der Schriften*.
- **1970s–1980s** — Minor updates to accompanying DIN publications and cross-references; the main classification unchanged.
- **1990s** — Proposals circulated in German type-trade press to update DIN for digital type and non-Latin scripts; did not result in a revised standard.
- **2000s** — Intermittent discussion of a revision; DIN working committee activity, no substantive output.
- **2010s–2020s** — Effectively frozen. Beuth Verlag continues to list the 1964 issue as current; no revision announced as of 2026-04-18.

The **non-revision** reflects both the institutional inertia of DIN standards (which tend to revise slowly) and the declining centrality of the German industrial-standards apparatus in a global digital type ecosystem dominated by Adobe Fonts, Google Fonts, MyFonts, and the major commercial foundries — none of which cite DIN as primary.

## When to use DIN 16518

- **German-language typographic documentation.** Catalogs, specimens, curricula in German-speaking contexts traditionally reference DIN.
- **Industrial / technical print specifications in Germany.** DIN carries contractual authority.
- **Comparative taxonomic analysis.** When comparing classifications (pedagogy, type-history research-survey), DIN is useful alongside Vox and Bringhurst.
- **Blackletter work.** DIN's five-way blackletter subdivision (Gotisch, Rundgotisch, Schwabacher, Fraktur, Fraktur-Varianten) is the reference for blackletter taxonomy in any language.

Conversely, **avoid DIN 16518 as the primary classification when**:

- The audience is English-speaking or international (use Vox-ATypI or Bringhurst).
- Sans-serif subdivision matters (Gruppe VI is under-specified — use BS 2961's four-way Lineal split).
- Non-Latin typography is in scope (Gruppe XI is a placeholder — use script-specific conventions in `../scripts/`).
- Contemporary digital / screen-first type is the subject (DIN is a 1964 document; use looser contemporary genre buckets).
