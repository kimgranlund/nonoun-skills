# Thibaudeau classification — typography reference (stub)

The **Thibaudeau classification** is a 1921 French typographic taxonomy proposed by **Francis Thibaudeau** (1860–1925) in *La Lettre d'Imprimerie*. It divides typefaces into **four categories** based on **terminal silhouette** — the shape of the serif (or its absence). Superseded internationally by Vox-ATypI 1954 (which extended Thibaudeau's four categories to nine), it survives today as a historical footnote in French typographic education and as the acknowledged ancestor of every subsequent classification.

This file is a **stub** — a brief orientation, a pointer to the original treatise for readers who need depth, and a clear cross-reference to Vox and Bringhurst as the contemporary classifications actually in use.

---

## Origin

**Francis Thibaudeau** (1860–1925) was a French typographer active in the late-19th and early-20th-c. Paris type trade. He worked at the **Renault foundry** (Fonderie G. Peignot et Fils / Deberny & Peignot predecessor lineage — the genealogy is tangled; sources variously place Thibaudeau at Renault, at Peignot, or at the Fonderie Peignot that later merged with Deberny to form Deberny & Peignot in 1923). He authored foundry specimens and design essays; his major published work is the two-volume *La Lettre d'Imprimerie* (Paris, 1921), followed by *Manuel français de typographie moderne* (1924).

*La Lettre d'Imprimerie* is a **comprehensive treatise on the printed letter** — covering anatomy, history, metal-type manufacture, composition, and classification. The classification chapter proposes the four-category system summarized below; Thibaudeau himself emphasizes silhouette (specifically the shape of the terminal, which he calls the **empattement** — French for "footing" or "base") as the primary sorting criterion.

---

## The four Thibaudeau categories

Thibaudeau's classification is **silhouette-based** — categories sort faces by what the serif (or non-serif) looks like, independent of axis, contrast, x-height, or historical era. The four:

1. **Elzévir** — Serifed romans with **bracketed serifs** and stroke contrast. Named after the 17th-c. Dutch printer-publishers Elzevir (Louis, Bonaventure, Abraham and others) whose editions were printed with Dutch-Garalde types that came to exemplify "proper" book roman in French typographic culture. In contemporary terms, **Elzévir = Vox Garaldes + Réales combined** — every serifed roman from Garamond through Baskerville falls here.
2. **Didot** — Serifed romans with **hairline unbracketed serifs** and extreme stroke contrast. Named directly after the Didot family (Firmin Didot's late-18th-c. cuts being the canonical example). Equivalent to **Vox Didones**.
3. **Égyptienne** — Serifed romans with **slab (rectangular) serifs** and minimal stroke contrast. Named after the 19th-c. "Egyptian" slab tradition (no connection to Egypt — the name reflects post-Napoleon Egyptomania fashion). Equivalent to **Vox Mécanes**.
4. **Antique** — **Sans-serif** faces. In French typographic terminology, "antique" is the historical word for sans-serif (related to the American-English "Gothic" used for the same category — neither term has a historical-accurate meaning; both are 19th-c. trade names). Equivalent to **Vox Linéales**.

Thibaudeau arranges these as four silhouettes of the letter's foot: bracketed-serif (Elzévir), unbracketed-hairline-serif (Didot), slab-serif (Égyptienne), no-serif (Antique). The method is pedagogically elegant — any typeface can be sorted by looking at one feature — and Thibaudeau presents them as mutually exclusive buckets.

### Method

The Thibaudeau method:

- **Silhouette-based.** One feature (the terminal / empattement) determines category. Contrast, axis, x-height, width, proportion, and era are secondary.
- **Four disjoint buckets.** Every face fits exactly one. The method is taxonomically clean even if typographically reductive.
- **Pre-typographic-anatomy-terminology vocabulary.** Thibaudeau writes before "axis," "x-height," and "aperture" became standard French typographic vocabulary. His descriptive language relies on metaphors and direct observation — "le pied" (the foot), "l'empattement" (the footing), "le filet" (the hairline).
- **Teachable in minutes.** A novice can sort 20 specimens into Elzévir / Didot / Égyptienne / Antique quickly; the method survives as a quick-triage tool even today.

### Anchors

Using Thibaudeau's categories on contemporary digital type:

- **Elzévir**: Garamond, Bembo, Caslon, Baskerville, Times New Roman, Sabon, Minion, Georgia, Source Serif — everything Vox splits across Humanes, Garaldes, Réales, and (arguably) the Baroque Dutch-English tradition.
- **Didot**: Didot, Bodoni, Walbaum, Modern No. 20, Scotch Roman — Vox Didones.
- **Égyptienne**: Rockwell, Clarendon, Courier, Memphis, Serifa, Archer, Roboto Slab — Vox Mécanes.
- **Antique**: Helvetica, Futura, Gill Sans, Frutiger, Inter, Roboto, Akzidenz-Grotesk — Vox Linéales (all four sub-classes, undifferentiated).

## Why it matters historically

Thibaudeau is the **acknowledged ancestor** of modern typeface classification:

- **First systematic French-language taxonomy.** Before Thibaudeau, French foundries sorted type by foundry-internal numbering schemes, trade-fashion labels, or ad-hoc stylistic groupings. Thibaudeau proposed the first method by which any foundry could sort any face using a shared vocabulary.
- **Direct precursor to Vox-ATypI.** Maximilien Vox's 1954 *Pour une nouvelle classification des caractères* **explicitly extends Thibaudeau** — Vox keeps the silhouette-informed spirit but adds historical / cultural distinctions (Humanes vs Garaldes vs Réales where Thibaudeau has only Elzévir; Scriptes vs Manuaires vs Fractures where Thibaudeau has nothing). Vox credits Thibaudeau in the 1954 essay as the foundation he is building on. Without Thibaudeau, there is no Vox; without Vox, there is no ATypI classification, no BS 2961, and arguably no contemporary classification vocabulary at all.
- **Cited in every serious French-language history of type classification.** Nicolas Taffin, Anne Cuneo, Roxane Jubert, and others treat Thibaudeau as the starting point of any discussion about how type gets classified in French typography.
- **Referenced in the broader classification literature.** Catherine Dixon's *A Description Framework for Typefaces* (ATypI Copenhagen 2001; Central Saint Martins PhD 2002) draws on Thibaudeau's terminal-focused approach as one historical input into her more recent multi-axis framework. Robin Kinross's *Modern Typography* notes Thibaudeau as the first major French taxonomic work.

## Limitations

Why Thibaudeau was superseded:

- **Four categories insufficient for modern range.** No blackletter category (Gebrochene / Fractures); no script category (Scriptes); no casual hand (Manuaires); no glyphic (Incises); no display / ornamental; no non-Latin. Thibaudeau's 1921 taxonomy covers roman + slab + sans and nothing else.
- **Conflates Garalde and Transitional.** Both go to Elzévir; the real stylistic difference between Garamond (1540s) and Baskerville (1757) is erased.
- **Conflates Grotesque, Geometric, and Humanist sans.** All go to Antique; Akzidenz-Grotesk, Futura, and Gill Sans end up in one bucket — the same weakness DIN 16518 would inherit 43 years later.
- **Silhouette-only.** Ignores axis (which distinguishes Humanes from Garaldes), contrast gradient (which distinguishes early-Didot from late-Didot from Bodoni-Didot), proportion, voice, era. Modern classification recognizes that serif shape alone is not enough.
- **Reductive where modern classification demands nuance.** A neo-humanist sans like Optima or a reverse-contrast face like Karloff cannot meaningfully be sorted by terminal silhouette alone.
- **Pre-electronic.** The taxonomy predates phototype, digital, variable fonts, and variable-axis-era experiments. Faces that shift category along an `opsz` or `CASL` axis are unrepresentable.

**Superseded by Vox-ATypI 1954** (and subsequent revisions in 1962, 1967, and 2010). Every contemporary classification — Vox, BS 2961, DIN 16518, Bringhurst — has more categories and finer distinctions than Thibaudeau's four.

## Contemporary status

In 2026, Thibaudeau's classification is:

- A **historical footnote** in English-language typographic discourse. English-language writers (Bringhurst, Lawson, Blackwell, Kinross, McLean) mention Thibaudeau as Vox's predecessor but do not use the Elzévir / Didot / Égyptienne / Antique vocabulary for contemporary sorting.
- **Occasionally referenced in French typographic education.** École Estienne (Paris) and ENSAD (École nationale supérieure des arts décoratifs) teach Thibaudeau as part of classification history; French-language typographic manuals routinely acknowledge the genealogy.
- **Occasionally cited when discussing classification methodology.** Catherine Dixon's ATypI papers and PhD thesis use Thibaudeau as one input to her description-framework research-survey; discussions of what any classification should do frequently start with Thibaudeau's silhouette-only approach as the simplest possible case.
- **Not used in contemporary foundry catalogs, type-design practice, or UI/web typography.** Adobe Fonts, Google Fonts, MyFonts, Typographica, I Love Typography, and every foundry of note uses Vox / Bringhurst / informal genre buckets.

## Why this skill covers Thibaudeau as a stub

Thibaudeau appears in this classification axis for three reasons:

- **Historical context.** Understanding Vox-ATypI requires understanding what it extended; reading Thibaudeau first clarifies what Vox added.
- **Occasional references in French-sourced typographic texts.** If a user is reading a French typography book or specimen that cites "classification Thibaudeau" or "Elzévir / Didot / Égyptienne / Antique" as category names, this file provides the cross-reference to contemporary terminology.
- **Complete classification axis.** The four classifications in widest reference (Vox, Bringhurst, DIN, Thibaudeau) form a natural set; omitting the ancestor of the other three would be an incomplete axis. Covering Thibaudeau as a stub rather than a deep or medium reference reflects the actual contemporary use — enough detail to cross-reference, not more.
