Albers vs. the scientific color models

Albers (1963) explicitly rejected the prescriptive color science of his day. Yet CIELAB (1976), CIECAM02 (2002), and OKLab (2020) eventually quantified the very phenomena he was teaching by hand. The fields converged — perception-first practice and math-first models now agree about what's actually happening.

Albers said the eye lies. Math says: the eye reports something different than what's there.

Both statements are correct — and they're saying the same thing. A color's measured values (in any colorimetric space) describe its physical reflectance or emission. Its perceived values depend on adaptation, context, and the spatial arrangement of neighboring colors. Colorimetric models predict the physical side. Perception models like CIECAM and CAM16-UCS predict the appearance side. Modern OKLab and OKLCh are colorimetric models whose axes are calibrated to perceptual uniformity — they're the closest thing to "Albers in math form" we have.

OKLCh's hue-uniformity (a hue rotation produces no perceived lightness change) is essentially Albers' "stable hue under variation" complaint, solved. His exercise of finding a chip's apparent appearance against varied surrounds is now reproducible numerically — feed the surround into CIECAM16's viewing-conditions model and you get a quantitative appearance prediction.

Takeaway

  • Artists: the science finally caught up — and confirmed what your eye was telling you.
  • Designers: modern color spaces (especially OKLCh) deliver Albers' lessons mathematically.
  • UI engineers: use OKLCh because it predicts perception; use Albers exercises to verify the prediction holds in your UI.
