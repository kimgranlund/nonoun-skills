Who was Josef Albers?

"Color is the most relative medium in art."
— Josef Albers, Interaction of Color (1963)

A teacher first, painter second

Josef Albers (1888-1976) was a German-born artist whose enduring contribution is not a single painting but a method of teaching color. Trained at the Bauhaus (1920-1933), he taught beside Klee and Kandinsky. When the Nazis shut the Bauhaus, he emigrated to North Carolina's Black Mountain College (1933-1949) where his students included Robert Rauschenberg, Ruth Asawa, and Kenneth Noland. Eva Hesse later studied under him at Yale, where he chaired the design department from 1950-1958.

His book Interaction of Color (1963) was a teaching kit — a boxed set of color-paper exercises. The squares pictured above belong to his Homage to the Square series, begun in 1949 and continued until his death — over a thousand paintings in 27 years, all with the same composition. The colors did the changing. Painting after painting was an experiment.

Takeaway

  • Artists: the method is the gift; his paintings were classroom demos.
  • Designers: every "this color reads wrong in dark mode" was anticipated in his teaching.
  • UI engineers: he proved that tokens-in-context are a perception problem — before tokens existed.
