Afterimage and optical memory

Stare at a saturated red square for 20 seconds, then look at a white field. You'll see a green-cyan ghost where the red was. Albers used this exercise to demonstrate that direct color perception is unstable — the eye chases adaptation, the brain fills in opponents, and what you see at second 1 is not what you see at second 20.

The opponent process

Sustained exposure to a color fatigues the corresponding cone receptors. When you then look at white (which contains all wavelengths in roughly equal proportion), the fatigued receptors fire less and the unfatigued ones dominate — producing the perceived opponent: red→green, blue→yellow, magenta→green-cyan, etc. This is Hering's opponent-process theory (1892), still the standard model for explaining afterimages.

Albers used the exercise as a humility lesson: don't trust your first reading of a color, because your eye is already adapting to it. Look away, take a breath, come back — and recheck.

Takeaway

  • Artists: your eye is a moving target — adjust work over time, not in one sitting.
  • Designers: the tenth time a user sees the screen, it doesn't look like the first; design for the adapted eye.
  • UI engineers: saturated backgrounds over long sessions induce adaptation — measure usability after fatigue, not at first contact.
