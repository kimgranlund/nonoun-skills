Transparency and overlap illusions

Two opaque shapes overlap. The intersection region is given a third color. If C is the right perceptual midpoint of A and B, the eye reads the shapes as transparent, layered over each other. If C is the wrong value, the shapes read as flat and disconnected. Albers obsessed over finding the exact midpoint that triggers the illusion.

RGB midpoint vs OKLab midpoint

In RGB, the midpoint of two colors is simply ((a + b) / 2) per channel. This is what naive "average two colors" buttons produce. In perceptual color spaces like OKLab, the midpoint is computed after a nonlinear transform that aligns lightness with what the eye sees. The two midpoints can differ noticeably — and the eye prefers the OKLab one for transparency.

Click snap C to OKLab midpoint — the shapes start to read as transparent layers. Click snap C to RGB midpoint — the shapes flatten again. The exact same A and B; only C changes.

This is why color-mix(in oklab, ...) produces blends that feel transparent. mix-blend-mode: multiply rarely does — it's doing the wrong math for human perception.

Takeaway

  • Artists: there's exactly one right midpoint, and the eye knows it.
  • Designers: layered surfaces (drawer over content, overlay over photo) need OKLab-midpoint blending to feel transparent.
  • UI engineers: use color-mix(in oklab, A, B) for layered token combinations — not multiply, not raw averages.
