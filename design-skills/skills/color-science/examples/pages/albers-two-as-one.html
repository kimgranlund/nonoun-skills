Two colors appearing as one

The inverse exercise — harder than one-as-two, and more revealing. Two physically different chips can be made to look identical by placing each on a ground that pushes its appearance toward the other's. The eye merges what measurement keeps distinct.

ΔE_ok between chips: —

The trick: opposing surrounds pull the chips together

If chip A is slightly darker than chip B, place A on a dark ground (which makes it appear lighter via simultaneous contrast) and B on a light ground (which makes it appear darker). At the right distances, both chips read as the same middle value. The two are physically different — the ΔE_ok stays non-zero — but the eye refuses to see the difference.

This is harder than the one-as-two exercise because you're fighting two physical differences at once: the chips and the grounds both need to align. When it works, it's the most persuasive demonstration that "color" and "measured value" are not the same thing.

Takeaway

  • Artists: color is forgiving — the wrong pigment can be made right by context.
  • Designers: two different brand tokens can look identical in different roles; sometimes that's the goal.
  • UI engineers: legacy palettes can converge perceptually without code changes — adjust surrounding tokens, not the brand color.
