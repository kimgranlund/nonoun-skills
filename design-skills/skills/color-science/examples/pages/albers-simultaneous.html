Simultaneous contrast

The most fundamental Albers phenomenon. A chip on a dark ground appears lighter; on a light ground, darker. Against a saturated ground, it appears duller; against a neutral ground, more saturated. The eye amplifies difference.

Pick a chip and two grounds

One variable, big effect

Set the chip to a neutral mid-gray and the two grounds to the extremes (one near black, one near white). The same chip will read as a noticeably lighter swatch on the dark side and a darker swatch on the light side — while its hex value never changes. Try different chip colors. Try matching hues. The amplification effect persists across the entire color space.

The strength of the illusion depends on the lightness gap between the grounds and the proximity of the chip's value to either extreme. Mid-tones placed between two roughly-equal contrasts produce the largest perceptual swing.

Takeaway

  • Artists: every color you mix gets re-mixed by what surrounds it.
  • Designers: pick brand color last, after the surface system is locked.
  • UI engineers: a "safe" primary on light surface isn't safe on a colored card — verify each pairing.
