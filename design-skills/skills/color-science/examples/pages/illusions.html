Color illusions — where measurement disagrees with perception

Three classic illusions, each one showing that identical RGB values can look completely different depending on context. The proof is in the hex readouts under each image — those numbers really are the same.

1. Simultaneous contrast

Two identical gray squares on two different backgrounds. The square on the dark background looks lighter than the square on the light background — but the squares are pixel-identical.

Both squares: #888888

2. White's illusion

Gray bars overlaid on black-and-white stripes. The bar on the dark-striped side appears lighter than the bar on the light-striped side — even though both are identical gray.

Both bars: #888888

3. Helmholtz-Kohlrausch effect

All four cells below have the same luminance (Y) — pure 50% gray and saturated yellow / red / blue all at L*=53. But the colored cells look brighter than the gray, because chromatic content boosts perceived brightness independent of luminance. This is why OKLCh and HCT distinguish "lightness" (perceptual) from "luminance" (radiometric).

All four: OKLab L = 0.6 → CIELAB L* ≈ 53

What these illusions teach you about color systems

Color science distinguishes colorimetry (what's physically there) from color appearance (what your brain reports). The illusions above are violations of colorimetric identity — physically identical pixels look different.

Practical implication: contrast ratios computed from RGB values can mislead. APCA, the modern accessibility metric, accounts for spatial-frequency context and polarity — partial acknowledgment that perception isn't just measurement. Try APCA vs WCAG side by side.

For the deepest dive on simultaneous contrast in design, see Josef Albers's Interaction of Color (1963) — the canonical illustrated book on how context changes color.
